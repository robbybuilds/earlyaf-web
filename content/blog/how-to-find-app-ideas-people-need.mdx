Solo builders don’t usually fail because they can’t ship. They fail because they spend months on a *cool* idea that never showed up as a real, recurring problem in the wild.

This post is a method for finding app ideas people actually need — before you write code. It is written for **solo app builders**, not agencies or general marketers. Soft product note at the end; the method stands alone.

## Discovery beats invention

You don’t invent demand. You find repeated firsthand complaints and workarounds, then decide whether *you* can run a first experiment.

Lists of “100 SaaS ideas” are invention theater. What you want instead is **dated evidence**: who said what, when, and with what consequence.

## Where to look

### 1. App Store and Play reviews (1–2 stars)

Pick a category you might build in. Read critical reviews across several apps. When the *same* complaint appears in unrelated apps, that is a signal worth noting — not yet a build order.

### 2. Reddit and founder communities

Search for phrases people use when they are stuck: “wish there was,” “looking for a tool,” “frustrated with,” “is there anything that…”. Read comments as carefully as posts. Patterns across threads matter more than one viral rant.

### 3. Workarounds

Spreadsheets, Zapier glue, manual DMs, copy-paste rituals — people already paying with time. A painful workaround is often stronger evidence than a polite survey answer.

### 4. Search and trends (with a hard limit)

Search interest ≠ willingness to pay. Rising queries can mean curiosity, homework, or a problem people will not buy a fix for. Treat trends as context, never as a green light by themselves.

## An evidence bar (use this before you commit)

Borrow these rules before you fall in love with a direction:

- Separate **observation** (“three founders described X”) from **recommendation** (“you should build Y”).
- Prefer **independent firsthand** reports. Dates and provenance matter.
- A complaint alone is **not** a build recommendation.
- Rule of thumb (not magic): look for recurrence across conversations *and* a concrete consequence; prefer recent signals.

If you cannot write the evidence in one paragraph without inventing numbers, you are not ready to build.

## Solo-builder filter

Even a real problem may not be yours. Ask:

- Can I ship a useful first version with my skills and time?
- Do I know where these people already talk?
- What is the smallest marketing experiment that would tell me if anyone cares?

Useful research can still be useful research even when the answer is “not for me.”

## Checklist

Before you open a repo:

1. Name the problem in one sentence.
2. List at least a few independent, dated observations (reviews, threads, workarounds).
3. Note the consequence (time, money, risk, embarrassment).
4. Decide your first experiment — not your five-year roadmap.
5. Only then write code or CTAs.

## FAQ

### What’s the difference between an idea list and market research?

An idea list is invention. Market research is collecting dated evidence about problems, audiences, and reach — then deciding what (if anything) to build or test.

### How many complaints before it’s worth investigating?

There is no magic number. Prefer recurrence across independent conversations plus a clear consequence. One viral post is a story; a pattern is a lead.

### Is search volume enough to build?

No. Search interest is not willingness to pay. Pair it with firsthand complaints, workarounds, or competitor review patterns.

### How is this different from browsing idea catalogs?

Catalogs are useful for ambient inspiration. This method is about **evidence → decision → first experiment**. Early AF’s wedge is the research habit and a free daily idea — not a claim that we replace a full marketing hire or suite.
